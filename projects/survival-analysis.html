Survival Analysis of Lung Cancer Data

R · Python · Clinical time-to-event modeling

A comprehensive survival analysis spanning 12 time-to-event models — from classical non-parametric estimators through to deep learning — on the NCCTG lung cancer clinical trial dataset.

Time-to-event analysis is central to clinical and health-outcomes research: it answers not just whether an event happens, but when, while correctly handling censored patients who leave the study before the event. I built this project to work through the full spectrum of survival methods used in clinical data science, in both R and Python, and to understand how classical statistical approaches and machine-learning approaches compare on the same clinical data.

Dataset

The NCCTG Lung Cancer dataset — 228 patients with advanced lung cancer from the North Central Cancer Treatment Group trial, with clinically meaningful covariates including ECOG and Karnofsky performance scores, age, sex, weight loss, and caloric intake.

Methods covered

The project moves deliberately across the methodological spectrum rather than fitting a single model:

  • Non-parametric
    Kaplan–Meier estimator, Nelson–Aalen estimator, log-rank test
  • Semi-parametric
    Cox proportional hazards, stratified Cox, time-varying covariates, frailty models
  • Parametric
    Accelerated failure time (AFT) models via flexsurv
  • Machine learning & deep learning
    Random Survival Forests, gradient-boosted survival models, and DeepSurv (pycox)
  • Model comparison
    Prediction-error benchmarking across all approaches with the pec package

Why it matters

Survival analysis is the statistical backbone of clinical trials and epidemiology, and the classical methods (Kaplan–Meier, Cox) remain the language of medical research — while newer ML and deep-learning approaches offer flexibility for complex, high-dimensional data. Working across both, in R and Python, reflects the toolkit needed to move between a biostatistics and a machine-learning setting.
